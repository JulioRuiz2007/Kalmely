I Hadn't Slept on a Single Flight in Years — Here's the £39 Thing That Finally Fixed It

For a decade I landed everywhere a wreck. Then a colleague who flies every week showed me what she uses — and my first night flight since has been my best.

Independent customer account · 4 min read

I used to dread the night flights most.

Not the flying itself — the not sleeping. The cabin lights that never fully go off. The engine drone that sits behind your eyes for eight hours. The stranger in 24B whose elbow has opinions about your armrest. I'd board exhausted, close my eyes, and just… lie there. Awake. Doing maths on how few hours of "sleep" I might steal before the trolley came round again.

And then I'd land and lose the first day of the trip. Every time. The holiday I'd saved a year for, or the meeting I'd flown six hours for — gone, behind a fog of jet lag, because I simply could not switch off on a plane.

I told myself it was just me. That some people can sleep anywhere and some people can't, and I was the second kind.

It turns out it wasn't me. It was my kit.

I'd tried everything, and looking back, each thing solved exactly half the problem:

  • Eye masks blocked some of the light, but they pressed on my eyes, slid off the second I turned, and did nothing about the noise.
  • Earplugs muffled the engine, but they ached after an hour on my side and left the cabin lights blazing through my eyelids.
  • Sleeping pills knocked me out, sure — and left me groggy and stupid for the entire first day, which was the day I'd flown there for.

Nothing covered the light and the noise at the same time, comfortably, the whole flight. And that, it turns out, is the only thing that actually works.

The colleague who flies every week

I found out from Sophie, who's cabin-side somewhere most weeks for work and is annoyingly fresh every time she lands. I finally asked her how.

She pulled a soft mask out of her bag. It didn't look like much — until she turned it over. Instead of flat fabric, it had two raised, contoured cups over the eyes. And tucked flat inside, right where your ears are, two slim Bluetooth speakers.

"Blackout and sound," she said. "In one thing. I put on rain noise, and the next thing I hear is the captain."

I ordered one that week. It's called Kalmely.

Why the design actually matters

I'm not a gadget person, so I had it explained to me simply, and it stuck:

The 3D cups. Because they're raised, they block 100% of the light without touching your eyes. You can blink. You can wear it over lashes or lash extensions. There's no pressure, so it doesn't give you that dull ache a normal mask does after a couple of hours. Total darkness, zero contact.

The flat built-in speakers. They sit over your ears inside the mask — no earbuds to fall out, nothing jammed into your ear canal that hurts when you lie on your side. You pair your phone and play whatever switches your brain off: rain, white noise, a podcast, your own playlist. It doesn't cancel the engine so much as bury it under something your brain would rather listen to.

Light handled. Noise handled. Comfortable on your side. That's the whole problem, solved by one soft thing you put on your face.

Long-haul. The kind I'd normally write off.

I woke up because the cabin lights came up for landing. I'd slept. On a plane. I walked off feeling like a person instead of something the trip had chewed up, and I had my whole first day back.

I've since worn it on a 2-hour hop and a 13-hour stretch. Same result both times.
